What Happens If…?

Use these as group prompts. Have students predict the direction of the change before revealing the answer.

1. Add one more worker

The shop already has many workers but only two ovens. What will happen to total output? What about marginal product?

2. Add an oven

The kitchen is crowded and the ovens are constantly occupied. What happens if the owner adds another oven?

3. Better ordering technology

A new POS system cuts ordering errors and sends tickets directly to the kitchen.

4. Poor organization

Workers are capable, but nobody coordinates stations, breaks, prep, or order priorities.

5. Run out of cheese

The shop has plenty of labor and capital, but ingredients are limited.

6. Workers + capital together

The owner expects a huge Friday rush. Is adding workers alone enough?

Production Function 101

A production function is a model of the relationship between inputs and the maximum output a firm can produce with a given level of technology and organization.

Labor (L)Workers. In the pizza shop: order takers, cooks, prep staff, and boxers.
Capital (K)Tools and equipment used to produce. In the pizza shop: ovens, mixers, prep tables, refrigerators.
Materials (M)Raw inputs that are transformed into output: dough, cheese, sauce, toppings, boxes.
Technology / productivity (A)Methods and tools that let the same labor and capital produce more output.
Entrepreneurship / organization (E)Organizing resources, designing the workflow, spotting opportunities, making decisions, and bearing risk.
Output (Q)The finished good or service. Here: pizzas produced during one four-hour shift.
Important teaching point: entrepreneurship is real, but it is difficult to measure as a physical input. In this simulator it is represented as an organization/productivity multiplier. A real economist might estimate its effect indirectly through productivity, management quality, firm practices, or residual differences in output.

Short run vs. long run

Short run: at least one input is fixed. In this app, ovens are fixed while workers change. Long run: the firm has time to change all inputs, including capital.

Diminishing marginal product

At first, adding workers can create specialization, so total product rises at an increasing rate and marginal product rises. After a point, fixed capital becomes crowded. Total product may still rise, but at a decreasing rate because marginal product is falling. If the shop becomes severely overcrowded, marginal product can reach zero and then become negative, causing total product to fall.

Production and cost curves are connected

The production side and cost side tell the same story from different angles. When workers become more productive, the extra cost of producing another pizza falls. When diminishing marginal product sets in, marginal cost rises.

AVC is variable cost per unit of output. ATC is total cost per unit and equals AVC plus average fixed cost. MC is the change in total cost divided by the change in output. MC pulls the averages: when MC is below an average, it tends to pull that average down; when MC is above it, it tends to pull the average up.

Why this matters

The concept explains why “hire more workers” is not automatically the best production decision. The answer may instead be “add another oven,” “change the layout,” “improve the process,” or “fix the ingredient bottleneck.”
