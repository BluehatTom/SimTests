What do a, b, c, and d mean?

Demand: Qd = a − bP

a = the demand quantity intercept: the quantity consumers would demand at a price of $0. It sets the demand curve's horizontal position. b = the demand price-response coefficient: if b = 2, a $1 increase in price lowers quantity demanded by 2 units.

Supply: Qs = c + dP

c = the supply quantity intercept: the algebraic quantity supplied at a price of $0. It sets the supply curve's horizontal position. d = the supply price-response coefficient: if d = 2, a $1 increase in price raises quantity supplied by 2 units.

Graph note: because economics usually puts Price on the vertical axis and Quantity on the horizontal axis, a and c are quantity-axis intercepts—not the vertical price intercepts. The vertical intercepts are a/b for demand and −c/d for supply.

Understanding responsiveness and elasticity

Responsiveness is the broad idea: how strongly buyers or sellers react when price changes. Elasticity is the formal percentage measure of that responsiveness. That is why introductory textbooks often use “responsive” and “elastic” almost interchangeably in their verbal explanations.

The visual rule of thumb is useful: on the usual economics graph, with Price on the vertical axis and Quantity on the horizontal axis, a flatter (more horizontal) demand or supply curve is generally described as more elastic, while a steeper (more vertical) curve is generally described as more inelastic. The limiting cases make the idea especially clear: a horizontal demand curve is perfectly elastic, while a vertical demand curve is perfectly inelastic.

Why that rule works here: in Qd = a − bP, a larger b means quantity changes more for each $1 change in price. When the equation is drawn with P vertically and Q horizontally, larger b makes the demand curve flatter. Likewise, a larger d makes the supply curve flatter. So b and d are reasonable visual indicators of responsiveness.

But slope and elasticity are not exactly the same measurement. Slope response uses the original units: “2 more units for a $1 price change.” Elasticity standardizes that response into percentages: “quantity changes by 1.4% when price changes by 1%.” Because elasticity is unit-free, it can be compared across products measured in very different units.

For the linear equations used here, point elasticity is |Ed| = b × P/Qd for demand and Es = d × P/Qs for supply. Demand elasticity is technically negative because price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, but introductory economics normally discusses its absolute value.

The most important qualification: one straight-line demand curve has the same slope everywhere, yet its elasticity changes as you move along it because P/Q changes. On a linear demand curve, the upper-left portion is elastic, the midpoint is unit elastic, and the lower-right portion is inelastic—even though the curve never changes slope.

So when textbooks say “the flatter curve is more elastic,” they are using a very helpful comparative visual shortcut, especially when comparing curves drawn on the same axes and around the same price/quantity region. It should not be read as “slope equals elasticity.” Axis units, scale, and the point on the curve also matter.

Interpretation: elasticity greater than 1 = elastic (quantity changes by a larger percentage than price); elasticity less than 1 = inelastic; elasticity equal to 1 = unit elastic.

Point versus midpoint elasticity: this explorer reports point elasticity at a specific price and quantity. When a textbook calculates elasticity between two observed points, it often uses the midpoint (arc elasticity) formula so the percentage change does not depend on which direction you move between the two points.

Student takeaway: “more horizontal = more elastic” is a good visual intuition. The elasticity number is the precise percentage-based measure that tells us how elastic the curve is at a particular point or over a particular interval.
